Living at Waterfront Square, Philadelphia

October 1, 2026

Waterfront Square is a gated condominium community of three towers, the Reef, the Regatta and the Peninsula, on the Delaware River at 901 N. Penn Street, between Northern Liberties, Fishtown and Old City. Living there means river and skyline views, a full set of on-site amenities and quick access to I-95, but the experience of any one unit depends on its tower, floor, exposure, layout, parking and the building's finances, so no two purchases are the same. Naomi has closed more than 30 sales and leases at Waterfront Square since 2018, according to Bright MLS records as of September 2026.

I know Waterfront Square inside and out. I have sold units there, and I have seen how differently two apartments in the same building can live. This guide is what I tell people before they tour: where the complex sits and how you get around from it, what the association itself says it offers, how units differ from each other, and the questions you need answered before you sign anything. It also covers the other side of the table, because if you already own at Waterfront Square, the question is how your unit stands against every other one for sale in the same complex.

One rule runs through the whole page. Condo fees, pet rules, rental rules, reserves, special assessments and parking arrangements change. A number that was right last year may be wrong today, which is why you will not find them here. Ask me for the current answers and I will get them for the specific unit you are looking at.

Where Waterfront Square is, and how you get around

The address is 901 N. Penn Street, Philadelphia, PA 19123. The Philadelphia Inquirer places the complex at Columbus Boulevard and Spring Garden Street, right on the Delaware River. The three towers

According to the association, Waterfront Square is made up of three towers, the Reef, the Regatta and the Peninsula, with 484 units ranging from one to three bedrooms. It was not always meant to stop at three. The Inquirer reported in 2013 that the project was planned as five residential towers, and the two that would have completed the plan were never built as condominiums.

The Reef is the newest. The Inquirer reported that the Regatta and the Peninsula were completed during the housing boom and that the Reef's completion was delayed until April 2009. It described the Reef as a 22-story tower with 179 units. City of Philadelphia property records list 2009 as the year built for that tower's units. The Regatta and the Peninsula date from the middle of the 2000s. Published completion years for those two differ between sources, and I would rather tell you that than print a year I cannot stand behind.

The design of the site matters to how it lives. The association says the complex was designed by Wallace Roberts & Todd and that the towers are arranged around a landscaped park built as a green roof, which also provides walking and recreational space for residents. In practice, that means your view, your light and even your walk to the garage depend on which tower you are in and which side of it faces the park, the river or the city.

Why the tower matters before the unit

  • Each tower has its own age, its own systems and its own lobby. A unit in the newest tower and a unit in one of the two older towers can carry different maintenance histories.
  • Floor plans, balcony depth and window lines differ by tower and by stack. A layout that works in one tower may not exist in another.
  • Your view is set by the tower and the exposure: some units face the river and the Ben Franklin Bridge, some face the Center City skyline, some face the grounds.
  • Buyers comparing sold prices should compare within the same tower and, where they can, the same stack and similar floor, rather than across the whole complex.

Two units in the same building can be completely different

This is the single biggest misunderstanding I see with Waterfront Square. People assume that because the address is the same, the units are interchangeable, and that a price per square foot from one sale tells you what another unit is worth. It does not. What to ask before you buy at Waterfront Square

Pennsylvania gives condo buyers a real tool here. Under the Uniform Condominium Act, 68 Pa.C.S. 3407, a seller who is reselling a unit must give the buyer copies of the declaration, the bylaws, the rules and regulations and a resale certificate. The association must produce the certificate within ten days after the owner asks for it. And the buyer can void the purchase contract until the certificate has been provided and for five days afterward, or until settlement, whichever comes first. Use those five days. This is the checklist I work through with every buyer:

Ask Naomi for the current answers

  • Fees and what they cover. The monthly common expense assessment for this unit, any other fees, and which services and utilities the fee includes. The certificate must state the assessment and other fees.
  • Reserves. The amount of reserves for capital expenditures, and any portion set aside for a specific project. The certificate must state both.
  • Planned work. Capital expenditures the association has proposed for the current fiscal year and the next two. The certificate must list them.
  • Assessments. Any special assessment currently due, and whether one has been discussed or approved but not yet billed. Read recent board meeting minutes where you can get them.
  • Finances and lawsuits. The latest balance sheet, income and expense statement and operating budget, and any judgments or pending suits involving the association.
  • Insurance. What the master policy covers and what you need to insure yourself. The certificate must describe coverage provided for the benefit of owners.
  • Pet and rental rules. Current restrictions, registrations and any leasing limits, from the rules and regulations and the declaration, not from a listing description.
  • Parking type. Whether the space is licensed, assigned, deeded or leased, whether it transfers with this sale, and what it costs.
  • Which tower and which exposure. Which tower the unit is in, which way it faces, and what that means for light, views and systems.
  • Alterations and violations. Whether the board knows of any alteration to the unit that breaks the declaration, or any violation of governmental requirements. The certificate must answer both.

Flood maps and insurance

Waterfront Square sits directly on the Delaware River. Do not rely on anyone's word, including mine, about flood risk. Look up the address on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center at msc.fema.gov, the official public source for flood hazard maps under the National Flood Insurance Program, review the association's master insurance summary, including any flood coverage, and ask your own insurance agent what a unit policy should cover.

Beyond the condo documents, the rest of a Philadelphia purchase still applies: get pre-approved before you seriously shop, understand closing costs including the realty transfer tax, and review the unit's current taxes, assessed value and any abatement or exemption. My guide to buying a condo or co-op in Center City Philadelphia walks through the condo process in detail, and the payment calculator helps you see how a monthly fee changes what you can comfortably carry.

Inspections deserve a word too. In a high-rise, the inspection covers your unit and its systems, while the association is responsible for the common elements. Knowing where that line falls for this unit, including windows, balcony and heating and cooling equipment, is part of reading the declaration, and I can connect you with inspectors who work in Philadelphia high-rises.

Selling a unit at Waterfront Square: positioning a unit against the rest of the building

If you own at Waterfront Square, your competition is not just the market. It is the other units for sale in the same complex, often in the same tower, sometimes on the same stack. Buyers see all of them side by side, and they will compare yours against every one. Pricing at Waterfront Square is building by building, and inside the complex it is tower by tower and stack by stack.

Price against the right comparables

A neighbor's sale is not your price. Buyers do not see homes that way. They compare everything available to them at that moment. I look at recent sales and current competition in your tower first, then adjust for floor, exposure, layout, balcony, parking and condition. The goal is to position the unit correctly from the first day, because the first few weeks on the market carry the most attention. When a unit looks overpriced next to three similar listings in the same complex, buyers often do not make a lower offer. They simply move on.

Lead with what makes your unit different

  • Views. If you face the river or the bridge, the photography, the video and the showing times should prove it. Sunrise over the Delaware is a selling point you can schedule.
  • Layout and light. A floor plan in the listing lets buyers compare your layout honestly against others.
  • Updates. A renovated kitchen or bath separates a unit from the original-finish units around it. Document what was done and when.
  • Parking. Have the paperwork showing exactly how your space is conveyed and whether it transfers.

Prepare the condo documents before buyers ask

Buyers and their agents will look hard at fees, reserves, assessments, insurance and the rules. Because the association has ten days to produce the resale certificate and the buyer's five-day window only starts once they receive it, I request it early so it is ready, and I review it with you so there are no surprises in the middle of a contract. If a special assessment is pending or planned, we talk about how to handle it before it becomes an obstacle.

Decide where to spend money, and where not to

I walk through the unit with you and recommend what is worth doing before the photographs, such as paint, cleaning, lighting or staging, and just as importantly what is not. I can connect you with contractors, cleaners and stagers who know the building's rules for access, elevators and deliveries. Frequently asked questions

Where is Waterfront Square in Philadelphia?

Waterfront Square is at 901 N. Penn Street, Philadelphia, PA 19123, on the Delaware River waterfront near Spring Garden Street and Columbus Boulevard. The association describes it as a gated riverfront enclave near Northern Liberties, Fishtown and Old City, close to I-95, the Ben Franklin Bridge and I-676. The SEPTA Spring Garden station on the Market-Frankford Line is a short walk west along Spring Garden Street.

How many towers are at Waterfront Square?

Three. The association lists the Reef, the Regatta and the Peninsula, with a total of 484 one to three bedroom units. The project was originally planned as five residential towers, and the remaining two were never built as condominium towers.

When was Waterfront Square built?

The Reef, the newest of the three towers, was completed in April 2009, according to The Philadelphia Inquirer, and City of Philadelphia property records list 2009 as the year built for its units. The Regatta and the Peninsula came first, in the middle of the 2000s. Published completion years for those two towers differ between sources, so confirm the year for a specific tower before relying on it.

What should I ask before buying a condo at Waterfront Square?

Ask for the Pennsylvania resale certificate and read it closely: the current monthly assessment and what it covers, other fees, the reserves for capital expenditures, any capital expenditures proposed for the current and next two fiscal years, pending lawsuits, insurance coverage, and the rules and regulations. Then ask about recent or planned special assessments, pet and rental rules, how the parking space is conveyed, and which tower, floor and exposure the unit has. These answers change over time, so ask Naomi for the current ones.

Is parking included at Waterfront Square?

It depends on the unit and on how the space is conveyed. The association describes a covered parking garage behind a staffed, gated entry, but a buyer should never assume what comes with a particular unit. Confirm in writing whether the space is licensed, assigned, deeded or leased, whether it transfers with the sale, and what it costs. Naomi can verify the current arrangement for any unit you are considering.

What does a Pennsylvania condo resale certificate include?

Under 68 Pa.C.S. 3407, a seller of a Pennsylvania condominium unit must give the buyer the declaration, bylaws, rules and a certificate covering items such as the monthly common expense assessment, any unpaid or special assessment, other fees, proposed capital expenditures for the current and two following fiscal years, reserves, the latest balance sheet and budget, judgments and pending suits, and insurance. The association must provide it within ten days of the owner's request, and the buyer may void the contract until the certificate is provided and for five days after, or until settlement, whichever comes first.

Is Waterfront Square in a flood zone?

Look it up rather than assume. Enter the address on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center at msc.fema.gov to see the current effective flood map for the site, and ask the association for its master insurance summary, including any flood coverage, as part of your due diligence. Then talk to your own insurance agent about coverage for your unit.
