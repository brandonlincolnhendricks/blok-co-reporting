What People Get Wrong About Philadelphia Real Estate

October 1, 2026

The biggest thing people get wrong about Philadelphia real estate is treating it as one market. It isn't: value here changes from neighborhood to neighborhood, building to building and sometimes block to block, so the shortcuts that work elsewhere, like price per square foot or a neighbor's sale price, can point you in the wrong direction.

Below are the seven misconceptions I hear from buyers and sellers, why each one misleads in this city, and what I suggest doing instead. After that, I cover the objections that come up in almost every conversation and how I answer them.

I work with buyers and sellers across Philadelphia, in condominiums, rowhomes, twins, single-family homes and co-ops, and with renters as well. Whatever the property, the same handful of assumptions tend to shape people's decisions before we ever walk through a door. None of them is foolish. Many come from reading national headlines, from how real estate works in other cities, or from one well-meaning conversation with a friend. They just don't hold up well in a city built the way Philadelphia is.

For each one I have set out the belief, why it is wrong here, and what to do instead. If you are buying, my guide to buying a home in Philadelphia goes deeper on the process. If you are selling, start with my guide to selling a home in Philadelphia.

Seven misconceptions about Philadelphia real estate

1. Philadelphia is one market

The belief. Philadelphia has a price, a trend and a pace, and whatever the headline says about the city applies to the home you are buying or selling.

Why it is wrong in Philadelphia. Real estate here can change dramatically from neighborhood to neighborhood, building to building and sometimes block to block. Two homes only a few streets apart can have very different values, a different feel day to day and different resale potential. Rittenhouse, Society Hill, Washington Square West, Old City, Fitler Square, Queen Village, Fairmount and the Art Museum area each have their own personality and way of life, and within each of them there are little streets and tucked-away blocks that behave differently from the main thoroughfares a few steps away. For condominiums, the same is true building by building, and even within one building.

What to do instead. Narrow the question before you trust an answer. Instead of asking what homes cost in Philadelphia, ask what comparable homes have sold for on this block and the blocks around it, or in this building and the buildings like it, recently. Walk the blocks you are considering at different times of day. My neighborhood guides are a starting point for the housing stock, parks, transit and landmarks in each part of the city, and from there we can go street by street.

2. Price per square foot tells you what a home is worth

The belief. Divide the price by the square footage, compare it with the listing down the street, and you know whether a home is a good deal.

Why it is wrong in Philadelphia. Buyers tend to lean on price per square foot far too heavily. In Philadelphia, value is much more nuanced. Parking, outdoor space, taxes, condo fees, views, floor level, condition, location within a neighborhood and even the particular block can each have a significant effect on value, and none of them shows up in a square-foot figure. Two condos of the same size in the same building can be very different homes depending on floor, exposure, views, layout, balcony, parking, monthly fees and even the building systems. Two rowhomes of the same size a few doors apart can differ in roof deck, yard, basement, mechanicals and the condition of the masonry.

What to do instead. Use price per square foot as one rough input, never as the answer. Compare homes on the features that actually drive value, line by line, and look at taxes and monthly costs alongside price. On taxes, the City's own tools are the place to check the facts. The City of Philadelphia states that the Homestead Exemption, available to owners who live in the property as their primary residence, reduces a property's assessed value by $100,000, and that property tax abatements exempt all or part of an improvement for a set number of years. Whether a particular buyer qualifies, and what a particular bill will be, are questions for the City's records and your own advisors, but they belong in the comparison from the start. You can look up a property's assessment on the City's Atlas property tool.

3. Every condo building is basically the same

The belief. A two-bedroom condo is a two-bedroom condo. Pick the unit you like, and the building is just the wrapper.

Why it is wrong in Philadelphia. The building matters tremendously. Financial health, management, assessments, amenities, parking, pet policies, rental policies and monthly fees can all affect both your experience living there and your ability to resell later. Some amenities are used every day; others simply look good in photographs. When you buy a condominium, you are buying into an association and its decisions, not just the rooms inside your front door.

Questions I help buyers ask about any condo building

  • How healthy are the association's finances, and what do the reserves look like?
  • Are there any current or planned special assessments?
  • What does the monthly fee cover, and how has it changed over time?
  • How is the building managed, and how responsive is management?
  • What are the rules on pets, rentals, renovations and moving in?
  • What does the association's insurance cover, and what do you need to insure yourself?
  • How is parking handled, and what exactly comes with this unit?
  • What have units in this building sold for recently, and how does this unit compare on floor, exposure and layout?

Fees, policies, assessments and reserves change. I never rely on last year's answer, and neither should you. Ask me for the current figures on any building you are considering.

What to do instead. Evaluate the building with the same care as the unit. Read the association documents, look at the finances, understand the rules before you fall in love with the view, and compare the building with its alternatives, not just the unit with other units. My guide to buying a condo or co-op in Center City walks through that due diligence step by step.

4. My neighbor's sale sets my price

The belief. The house next door sold for a certain price, so mine should sell for the same or more.

Why it is wrong in Philadelphia. Buyers don't see homes that way. They compare everything available to them at that moment, not what sold last spring. Your neighbor's home may have had a finished basement, a renovated kitchen, better photography, a different season or far less competition when it went on the market. Condition, presentation, photography, pricing, competition and timing all matter. It is one of the reasons one rowhome can sell quickly while a seemingly similar home a few doors away struggles.

What to do instead. Treat a neighbor's sale as a data point, then look at the whole picture: other recent sales nearby, the homes a buyer will see alongside yours today, and an honest comparison of condition and presentation. If you want a starting number, you can request a home value estimate, and then we can walk through the property together and refine it.

5. Price it high and see what happens

The belief. You can always come down later, so start high and let buyers negotiate.

Why it is wrong in Philadelphia. Today's buyers have tremendous access to information. When a property appears overpriced, they may not make a lower offer. They may simply move on. The first few weeks on the market can be incredibly important, because that is when the buyers who have been waiting for a home like yours see it for the first time. If the price keeps them away, the listing ages, and a later reduction does not always bring them back with the same energy.

What an overpriced launch can cost

  • Buyers who would have been interested at the right price never book a showing.
  • Your home helps sell the competing homes that look like better value beside it.
  • Each week on the market invites buyers to wonder what is wrong with it.
  • You may end up negotiating from a weaker position than if you had priced it correctly at the start.

What to do instead. Position the home correctly from the beginning. That means pricing against recent sales and current competition, and preparing the property so it shows well from the first day. I walk through every home with the seller and recommend where to spend money and, equally important, where not to. My seller's guide covers pricing, preparation and marketing in detail.

6. Real estate is purely rational

The belief. Price, square footage and comparable sales decide everything. Feelings have nothing to do with it.

Why it is wrong in Philadelphia. Both buyers and sellers sometimes underestimate how emotional real estate is. A home is worth what the market will support, but people still make decisions based on how a home feels, how it is presented and whether they can picture themselves living there. Choosing a home is not just about bedrooms, bathrooms and square footage. It is about your life. Can you walk to your favorite restaurant? Where will you walk your dog? How easy is it to get to the airport or 30th Street Station? Where will your guests park? Can you walk along the river in the morning? Will this neighborhood still feel right for you three years from now?

What to do instead. Respect both sides. Sellers should invest in presentation, because light, cleanliness, staging and good photography shape how a buyer feels in the first minute. Buyers should let the feeling guide them toward a home, then let the facts confirm it: the inspection, the numbers, the building documents and the comparable sales. If you are drawn to a classic rowhome, my guide to buying a Philadelphia rowhome covers what to look at beyond the charm.

7. You can understand Philadelphia online

The belief. Between listing sites, maps and street view, you can learn everything you need about a home and a neighborhood from a laptop.

Why it is wrong in Philadelphia. Perhaps the biggest misconception about Philadelphia real estate is that you can understand it entirely online. You can't. Philadelphia is a city of neighborhoods, blocks, buildings and even individual streets, each with its own personality. A listing tells you the bedrooms, the square footage and the asking price. It doesn't tell you how the light falls in the afternoon, how a block sounds on a weekday evening, which amenities residents actually use, or what buyers notice when they walk through a Philadelphia home that they overlook online.

What to do instead. Use online tools for what they do well. The City's Atlas is the right place to look up a property's public record, and listing sites are a good way to see what is available. Then come and see it in person, and walk the neighborhood, not just the house. Many of my clients relocate from New York City and Washington DC and start their search from a distance; my guide to moving to Philadelphia from New York or Washington DC explains how I help them see the city before they commit.

The objections I hear, and how I answer them

My job isn't to talk clients into buying or selling a home. It's to give them the knowledge, resources and confidence to make the right decision for them. In my experience, buyer objections usually come down to three things, and seller objections to three others.

Buyer objection 1: price

Buyers worry that they are paying too much, or that they cannot afford the part of the city they want. I start with the facts: what comparable homes have actually sold for, what the monthly cost looks like once taxes, insurance and any condo fee are included, and what closing will cost. I encourage buyers to get pre-approved before shopping seriously, and I can connect you with local lenders who can walk you through financing options and estimated monthly payments. From there I educate, strategize and negotiate creatively, using my local knowledge, my agent network and my database to look for opportunities that fit what you are trying to do. My goal is to help you pay a price you understand and can stand behind, not to promise a number.

Buyer objection 2: uncertainty about the neighborhood

For buyers who are new to Philadelphia or new to a part of it, the unknown is the hardest part. I answer it with time on the ground. We walk the blocks, look at the housing stock, find the parks, the river paths, the transit and the restaurants you will actually use, and compare how different streets feel. I also share what I know about the buildings, the little streets and the details you would not find searching online. You decide where you want to live. My role is to make sure you have seen enough to decide with confidence.

Buyer objection 3: fear of making the wrong decision

Buying a home is a large commitment, and the fear of getting it wrong is reasonable. I address it with due diligence rather than reassurance. That means a thorough inspection with specialists where needed, a careful review of condo documents when there is an association, confirming exactly what parking and other rights come with the property, and looking at the home through the eyes of a future buyer as well as today's. When you understand what you are buying, the decision becomes yours rather than a leap of faith. I stay hands-on from the first showing to settlement and beyond.

Seller objection 1: price

Sellers want to know what their home is really worth, and they do not want to leave money on the table. I answer that with smart pricing: a careful review of recent sales on the block or in the building, current competition, market conditions and the features that make your home different. Then I explain my reasoning so the number makes sense to you, not just to me. If you want to begin with an estimate, you can request one here.

Seller objection 2: cost

Selling costs money, and sellers are right to ask where it goes. Before a home goes on the market, I review estimated closing costs, transfer tax, mortgage payoff, commissions and other potential expenses with the seller, so you have a clearer picture of your estimated proceeds before you decide. I also recommend where preparation money is worth spending and where it is not, and I can connect you with contractors, cleaners and stagers when the work makes sense. The City of Philadelphia's Department of Revenue states the realty transfer tax rate as 4.578% of the sale price or assessed value of the property, plus any assumed debt, paid as 3.578% to the City and 1% to the Commonwealth. The City also states that the tax is usually split evenly between the buyer and the seller, but that this is not a legal requirement, and that the City has the right to collect the full tax from either party. In practice, who pays what is negotiable in the agreement of sale, so it belongs in the conversation before you sign, not at the settlement table.

Seller objection 3: timing

Sellers ask whether now is the right time, and whether they can line up a sale with their next move. There is no universal answer, which is exactly why it is worth a conversation. We look at your plans, what is happening on your block or in your building, and what competition you are likely to face. For sellers who have already left Philadelphia, I can handle the sale from start to finish, from preparing the property to coordinating showings to communicating with the attorneys and title professionals, so distance does not have to dictate your timing.

Across all six objections, my approach is the same: honest guidance, clear numbers, and hands-on service, communication and advocacy from the first conversation to closing.

Frequently asked questions

Is price per square foot a good way to value a Philadelphia home?

Only as a rough starting point. In Philadelphia, value depends on factors that price per square foot does not capture, including parking, outdoor space, real estate taxes, condo fees, views, floor level, condition, location within the neighborhood and the particular block. Two homes or condos of the same size can be worth very different amounts, so compare them feature by feature using recent sales nearby or in the same building.

Why do similar Philadelphia rowhomes sell for different prices?

Because rowhomes that look alike from the street often differ in ways that matter to buyers: condition, systems, masonry, roof deck or yard, parking, layout, natural light, taxes and the exact block. Presentation, pricing, competition and timing also shape the result. Philadelphia changes block by block, so a sale a few streets away is only one data point, not a price.

Should I price my house high and negotiate down?

Usually not. Buyers have extensive access to information, and when a home looks overpriced many of them simply move on rather than make a lower offer. The first few weeks on the market matter, because that is when the buyers who have been waiting see your home for the first time. Pricing correctly from the start, based on recent sales and current competition, generally gives a seller a stronger position than a later reduction.

Are all Center City condo buildings the same?

No. The building matters as much as the unit. Financial health, reserves, management, special assessments, amenities, parking, pet and rental policies, insurance and monthly fees vary from building to building and can affect both day-to-day living and resale. These details also change over time, so review the current association documents and finances for any building before you buy.

How long should a Philadelphia home take to sell?

It depends on pricing, condition, competition and timing, and it varies by neighborhood, block and building. A home that is priced correctly and well prepared for its first day on the market is in a stronger position than one that starts high and waits. The first few weeks matter a great deal, because that is when engaged buyers who have been waiting see a new listing.

Can I research Philadelphia neighborhoods online before buying?

Yes, as a first step. Listing sites show what is available, and the City of Philadelphia's Atlas tool shows public property records such as assessments. But online research cannot show you how a block feels, how the light falls, how a building is run or what you will walk to each day. Philadelphia is a city of neighborhoods, blocks and individual streets, so plan to see homes and walk the neighborhoods in person before you decide.

Who pays the transfer tax when you buy or sell a home in Philadelphia?

The City of Philadelphia states that the realty transfer tax is usually split evenly between buyer and seller, but that this is not a legal requirement and the City can collect the full tax from either party. The City lists the combined rate as 4.578% of the sale price or assessed value plus any assumed debt, made up of 3.278% for the City and 1% for the Commonwealth. The split is negotiable in the agreement of sale.
